A rotating inertia J with viscous friction c has G(s) = 1/(Js2 + cs). Close the loop with unity feedback, choose the controller in the first block, apply a step R(s) and read what each term of the controller does to the closed loop poles and the step response.

Reading the result

What each term does here

The plant already contains a pole at s = 0, a free integrator, because a torque produces a speed and the angle is the integral of the speed. With unity feedback that makes the loop type 1, so P alone already gives zero steady state error to a step. The proportional gain sets ωn = √(Kp/J), while ζωn = c/(2J) does not depend on Kp, so raising the gain adds overshoot without shortening the settling time.

The derivative term adds damping: in PD the characteristic equation becomes Js² + (c + Kd)s + Kp = 0. On its own, D has no position feedback at all. The s in Kds cancels the plant's pole at the origin, the loop only feeds back speed, and the output settles short of the step.

The integral term adds a second pole at the origin and raises the order to three. For a step it is not needed with this plant, and it costs stability: I alone is unstable for every KI > 0, and PI is stable only while c·Kp > J·KI (Routh). Its real job is to remove the error caused by a constant disturbance torque, which this model does not include.

What the model leaves out. The motor torque is unlimited, so large gains give responses a real actuator could not produce. With D in the controller a step in r produces an impulse in the controller output at t = 0 (derivative kick); real controllers filter the derivative or act on the measurement only. There is no sensor noise, no delay and no disturbance torque.
